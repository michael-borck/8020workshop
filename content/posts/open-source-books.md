I've written seven books. I didn't set out to call myself an author. I had opinions about how we teach programming and use AI, and writing them down was the most honest way to test whether they held up.

All seven are open source on GitHub, built with Quarto, and published as free KDP titles. Anyone can read them, fork them, or tell me where I'm wrong.

### The AI series

- **Conversation, Not Delegation.** The one I'm most opinionated about. AI is a thinking partner, not a magic button you press and walk away from. You get out what you put in. The book teaches intentional prompting as a method.
- **Partner, Don't Police.** For educators working out how to handle generative AI in business courses. Practical ways to integrate it instead of banning it. Banning AI from the classroom is like banning calculators: you can do it, but you aren't preparing anyone for reality.

### The Python series

- **Think Python, Direct AI.** A programming textbook that teaches Python and computational thinking with AI as a learning partner. The point is to let AI help you understand what your code does, not to let it write the code for you.
- **Code Python, Consult AI.** Python fundamentals framed around working with AI coding assistants. The goal is fluency, not dependency.
- **Ship Python, Orchestrate AI.** The production side: testing, packaging, DevOps, documentation. How to get from "it works on my machine" to deployed and maintained, which is the part that separates a script from a project.

### The web and tools

- **Build Web, Guide AI.** Web development with AI assistance, covering HTML, CSS, JavaScript, WordPress and React for business contexts.
- **Book Forge.** A tool, not a book. A cross-platform desktop app that generates complete books using AI, with multi-provider support. Built in TypeScript and Electron. It is the tool I wished existed while writing the other six.

### Why open source, why free

The point was never revenue. It was putting ideas into the world and seeing if they survive contact with other people. Open source means anyone can challenge the content, suggest improvements or adapt it for their own context. KDP means anyone can grab a copy without a paywall.

Writing a book sounds like a massive undertaking. It is if you are aiming for a traditional publishing deal. Quarto, GitHub and KDP shorten the distance between "I have an opinion" and "it's published" to something surprising. The writing is the hard part. Everything else is infrastructure, and the infrastructure is essentially free.
