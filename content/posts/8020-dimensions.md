The Pareto principle says you can get 80% of the results from 20% of the input. I've been guilty of misapplying it to myself, so I want to clear one thing up: you have to pick your dimension.

If you put in 20% of the time, 20% of the effort and 20% of the money, you are not getting 80% of the results. You get a dusty telescope in a shed and a 3D printer that has never been calibrated.

The 80-20 Workshop philosophy has always been about money. Spend 20% of the budget and get 80% or more of the results. Source a telescope mount on Facebook Marketplace instead of buying retail. Restore a vintage 3D printer instead of ordering the latest model. Choose the mid-range sensor over the flagship.

That equation has a cost, paid in a different currency: time, effort and skill. Professional results from modest gear are not magic. They demand deep learning, patient calibration and genuine craft. The money you didn't spend gets replaced by hours at the workbench, by understanding your equipment at a level that most people with premium setups never reach.

I've [written before](/posts/hyperfocus-and-guilt/) about how this feels in practice, the guilt of going all-in on one project. That deep investment isn't a violation of the philosophy. It's the currency that funds it.

## Pick your trade

The insight behind 80-20 isn't "do less of everything". It's "be strategic about *where* you economise and where you go all in".

For most makers the trade looks like this. Economise on gear: buy smart, source secondhand, learn what actually matters and what is marketing. Go all in on skill, because that is where the returns live. A skilled operator with a $500 setup will outshoot a beginner with a $5,000 rig every time.

You are not shrinking every input to 20%. You are reallocating. Less money, more skill. Less gear, more craft. The total investment might be just as large, but the mix is different.

## The trap of universal 80-20

If someone tells you they 80-20 their time, their money and their effort, ask to see their results. The Pareto principle is a lens for finding leverage on one axis, not a licence for half-measures across the board.

The maker who buys cheap gear and never learns to use it properly has not found the 80-20 sweet spot. They cut two corners at once and ended up with 20% results.

## The formula

In practice it looks like this: 20% of the money plus 100% focused skill development gets results that compete with, or beat, setups costing five times more.

You pick the dimension where optimisation makes sense and invest fully in the dimension that drives quality. So if you are deep in a project right now, fully absorbed, spending every evening in the workshop or behind the screen, you are not breaking the 80-20 rule.

You are the reason it works.
